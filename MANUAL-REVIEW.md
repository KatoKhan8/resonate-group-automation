# What a person still has to check

3,553 automated tests, a mutation audit, a network-blocked harness and an
AST invariant suite cover a great deal. This file is the remainder: the
things that are true or false for reasons no assertion can reach.

`UX-REVIEW.md` is the guided walkthrough — which screens, in which order.
This is the shorter, sharper list of *why* a human has to be the one looking.

---

## The rule that decides what belongs here

A test can assert that a number is counted from the same function twice. It
cannot assert that the number means what the sentence beside it says. Anything
that turns on **meaning, judgement, or how a stranger reads it** is below.

---

## 1. Does the copy say something true about us?

The claim resolver enforces that a sentence is *supported*. It cannot enforce
that the supported sentence is the one a reasonable person would write.

Open `/outreach/contact/demo-acme/sarah-jones` and read the planned messages
aloud. Then open **Claim diagnostics** and read the refusals.

Ask of each allowed claim:

- Would the named colleague recognise this description of what they did?
- If the prospect forwarded this sentence to that colleague, would the
  colleague confirm it without hesitating?
- Does the temporal phrase match the prospect's own memory of their inbox?
  ("recently" for a touch 22 days old is deliberately vague; "earlier this
  week" for the same touch would be a small lie they can check.)

A test can check that "earlier this week" only appears within the same ISO
week. It cannot check that a human reading it feels lied to.

---

## 2. Does the sequence read as one campaign or four?

`campaignqa` blocks a sender outside the team and a step with no target role.
It cannot tell you that four correctly-validated sequences arriving at one
company in one week read as an agency that does not talk to itself.

Open `/outreach/account/demo-acme` and read the **Account timeline** as a
person at Acme would experience it — in order, ignoring which screen produced
each row. Three people, two channels, four senders. Does it feel like one
company reaching out, or four?

The fatigue limits are the automated half of this question. The half above is
not a number.

---

## 3. Would you send this to a client?

Generate all four reports (`/reporting/client`) and read them end to end:

- Executive Report
- Resonate Outbound Performance Report
- Deep-Dive Performance Report
- Internal Operations Report

Tests assert that every rate carries its fraction, that no provider name
reaches a client-facing document, that meetings are named as untracked rather
than printed as zero, and that the funnel and the multichannel split count one
audience.

They cannot assert that the observations are *useful*, that the tone is right
for a client's leadership, or that a section which passes every check is
nonetheless the wrong thing to lead with.

Read the **Observations** page especially. It is deterministic — generated by
comparing counted values against stated thresholds — which makes it correct
and does not make it insightful.

---

## 4. Do the numbers survive being read by somebody who wants to argue?

Pick any figure on any screen and try to break it:

- Take **Contactable** from the executive report. Add the multichannel split's
  email groups. They must agree. (They disagreed until recently: the funnel
  printed the double-verified count under a sentence promising email-security
  screening too.)
- Take **Confirmed sent** and count the confirmed touches on the account
  screens. A confirmed touch is `push_marked`, `email_delivered` or
  `linkedin_connected`; `push_prepared` and `draft_approved` are intentions.
- Take any conversion rate and check its denominator is the stage above it,
  not the top of the funnel.

There is a test for each of these three. There is no test for the fourth thing
you will think of, which is why you should think of it.

---

## 5. Does a role see only what it should?

Tenancy is tested hard — `tests/test_tenancy_penetration.py` — and cross-tenant
reads return 404 rather than 403 so the boundary leaks nothing, not even
existence.

What a person has to judge: **whether a screen a role legitimately reaches
still shows it too much.** Sign in as `client@productive.test` and read every
screen in the nav. Nothing there should mention a supplier, a credit cost,
another workspace, or the operations channel.

A permission test proves the client may open the page. Only a reader can say
the page tells them something they did not buy.

---

## 6. Does an error say what to do next?

Force a few failures and read what comes back:

- Approve a campaign, edit its copy, approve again — the approval should go
  stale and say so rather than looking current.
- Open a URL for a record in another workspace — 404, no hint the record
  exists.
- Generate a report as a role that may read but not generate — the button
  should be absent, not present and failing.

A test asserts the status code. Whether the message leaves an operator knowing
their next move is a judgement.

---

## 7. Does the demo tell the truth about itself?

Every screen in `--demo` shows a `DEMO` marker and every generated PDF carries
"Fictional companies and people. Not a record of real outreach."

Check that the marker is present on any screen you would screenshot for a
prospect, and that it is legible rather than technically present.

---

## 8. What the browser pass cannot be automated to notice

Run through `UX-REVIEW.md` and watch for the classes of defect that only look
wrong:

- an identifier printed where a name belongs (`mark` under a panel saying
  "Mark Weber"; `john-smith` under a column saying "John Smith")
- a machine token printed where a sentence belongs (`awaiting_approval`,
  or a Python tuple repr)
- two numbers for one thing with nothing distinguishing them
- a control that is always disabled, or a screen nothing links to
- a placeholder wider than the field offering it

Each of these was found by looking, and each now has an invariant. The point
of the list is that the *next* one will also be found by looking.

## 9. Is the reply policy the one you would defend to a client?

The engine now does what `/replies/policy` says it does, which moves this
from "check the screen is honest" to "check the judgement is right". No test
can do that: every default here is a judgement, and the tests pin the
judgement rather than validating it.

Sign in as an operator and read `/replies/policy`, including the new
**What each reply does** table. Then ask, for each row:

- **Positive → hold the account.** Sarah's Thursday email stops because John
  started a conversation on Tuesday. Right for a six-person company; is it
  right for a two-thousand-person one, where John and Sarah may not know
  each other?
- **Not interested → colleagues carry on.** This is the row that changed
  most. One person declining no longer stops the account. Would you be
  comfortable if the person who declined was the CEO?
- **Left the company → sequence ends, not suppressed.** A dead address, not
  a removal request, so an operator can reopen it. Is that the line you
  want?
- **Unclassified → hold everything and ask.** The conservative default, and
  the one that costs the most in held sequences. Check `/replies` for how
  many are sitting there.

Three outcomes still need a person to name them - `wrong_person`,
`left_company`, `existing_client` - see `PRODUCT-GAPS.md` §3. `referral` is
now classified, and what it produces is a *mention* rather than an edge: read
a few in the task centre and check that the ones naming somebody we do not
hold really do say "a person has to decide who this is" rather than quietly
picking one. Classify a few
by hand in the Reply Center and watch what happens to the account, because
that is the path a real operator will use every day and the one no rule
covers yet.

One thing worth trying deliberately: send a reply that says "please remove
our whole company" through the demo and confirm every contact there goes
suppressed, not just the sender. There is a test for it. Watch it happen
anyway - this is the transition where being wrong is unrecoverable.

## 9b. How old may a verification be?

There is no answer in the code, and that is the point of this entry.
`src/verification.py` has no concept of a verdict expiring: two
independent PASS results satisfy the rule whether they were obtained this
morning or two years ago. Every other freshness question in this build -
research evidence, MX, account signals - has an ageing rule and reports
what it is.

Nobody has chosen a number, and choosing one is not a parser change. It
is a claim about how fast a mailbox goes away, and it costs re-verification
credits to honour.

**What to check:** export `/export/contacts.csv`, sort by
`oldest_evidence_days`, open the contact at the top and ask whether you
would send to that address today on the strength of it. If the answer is
no, the number you would have picked is what is missing.

A blank in that column means *undated*, not new. A verdict carried over
from before this module existed has no timestamp on it, and the code
refuses to stamp one - a two-year-old verification reported as today's
would be a confident wrong answer, which is worse than an absent one.
The contact page says the same thing in words.

## 10. Is a hand-entered signal actually evidence?

The one part of account intelligence no test can reach.

`signals.record` refuses a blank, refuses anything under twelve characters,
and refuses an engagement type. What it cannot do is tell the difference
between an observation and an impression. "They seem to be scaling up
fast" clears every check in the codebase and is not evidence: nobody can
quote it back, and nobody can check it later.

So this is a reading job. Open `/signals`, then any account with manual
signals on it, and read each one as a sentence somebody might have to
defend:

- Could this be **quoted back** to the prospect without embarrassment?
- Would a colleague be able to **go and check it** - a page, a post, a
  filing, a date?
- Is it what was **seen**, or what somebody **concluded** from it? "7 open
  delivery roles" is the first. "Hiring aggressively" is the second.
- Does the **date** mean when it happened, or when somebody noticed? The
  decay curve treats it as the first.

A signal that fails these does not merely sit there being weak. It scores.
It moves an account up a list, and it does so with the same weight as one
that would survive being read out loud.

Two related things to look at while you are there:

- **The score is a claim about attention, not about permission.** Find an
  account that is high-priority and not eligible - the demo has one - and
  confirm the screen says both, in that order, without the second reading
  like a footnote.
- **A signal is not permission to write the observation.** Nothing stops a
  person recording "raised a Series A" and a generator later being asked
  to mention it. `outreachclaims` is what decides that, and the boundary
  between "reason to prioritise" and "thing we may say" is one only a
  person reading the draft can confirm held.
